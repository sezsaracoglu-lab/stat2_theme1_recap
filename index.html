📜 The rules of the game

Twelve rules that govern all six topics. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. Classify before you calculate. The measurement level of your variable decides which tests are legal. Settle it first and most of the rest follows.
  2. Plot before you summarise. A mean reported without a look at the distribution is a guess. Shape, then centre, then spread — always that order.
  3. Name the skew after the tail. The long thin end gives the distribution its name, not the hump where the data pile up.
  4. Squaring is not optional. Deviations from the mean always sum to exactly zero, so they must be squared before they can be added up into a measure of spread.
  5. Divide by N − 1 for a sample. A sample sits closer to its own mean than to the population’s, so dividing by N would understate the spread.
  6. A z-score is a position, not a probability. It says how many SDs out you are. Turning that into a percentage of people takes the normal table, and the table assumes normality.
  7. Hypotheses are about populations. H₀ and Hₐ are written with μ and p, never with x̄ and p̂. There is nothing to hypothesise about a number you already measured.
  8. H₀ carries the equals sign. It is the specific claim, which is exactly why it is the one you can build a reference distribution around.
  9. The p-value assumes H₀ is true. It is the probability of data this extreme in the null’s world — never the probability that the null is true.
  10. “Not significant” is not “no effect”. Failing to reject is failing to convict. It is fully compatible with a real effect that the study was too small to find.
  11. Set α before you look. Choosing the significance level after seeing the result is not a test, it is a decoration.
  12. Same people twice means paired. Always. Ignoring the pairing throws away most of the study’s power for no gain at all.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • Say, for any number you are given, whether it describes a sample or claims something about a population.

  • Name a distribution’s shape, and predict how the mean and median will differ.
  • Compute and choose between the mode, the median and the mean.
  • Build a standard deviation from scratch: deviations, squares, SS, variance, root.
  • Convert a raw score to a z-score and back again.
  • Use the ±1 SD = 68% rule and the normal table to turn a z into a percentage.
  • Write H₀ and Hₐ for a research question, and justify one- or two-sided.
  • Identify the independent and dependent variables, and explain what random assignment buys.
  • Define Type I and Type II errors, α, β and power, and say how they trade off.
  • Run the six steps of a z-test end to end, and state the conclusion in words.

🏛️ The whole year in one paragraph

Last year had one shape to it. You measure something, and the first question is what kind of thing you measured — because that decides everything that is allowed afterwards. Then you describe what you have: the shape of the distribution, where its middle sits, how far it spreads. From the mean and the spread you get the z-score, which turns any score into a position on one universal ruler, and the normal curve turns that position into a percentage of people. At that point describing runs out, because you never wanted to know about your hundred students — you wanted to know about students. So you write down the claim of no difference, work out what results chance alone would produce if that claim were true, and ask how far out your actual result landed. If it landed somewhere chance almost never reaches, you reject the claim — knowing that if the claim were in fact true, this procedure would mislead you only 5% of the time. Every test in Course 2.1 is that same paragraph with different arithmetic in the middle.

🔍 Two reasons you are here

To analyse what you collected — and to read what other people claim

⬇ builds onnothing — this is the ground floor⬆ hands upthe split between describing and inferring
  • ▸Reason one: you collect data, and you have to analyse it.
  • ▸Reason two: you read other people’s claims, and you have to judge them.
  • ▸A results sentence has fixed parts — the test, the df, the statistic, the p.
🪝 memory hookAnalyse, and audit. You do both.

In words

Statistics is not one skill but two, and the second one is the reason the course is compulsory. The first is obvious: you will run studies, and the numbers that come out have to be turned into an answer. The second is quieter and matters more often — almost every paper you read for the rest of your degree reports its results as a sentence of symbols, and if you cannot read that sentence you are taking the author's word for what their own data said.

  • Every part is there to answer “how surprised should I be?”
  • The lecture’s own joke slides are the point: “significant” has a technical meaning that almost nobody uses correctly in conversation.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Never read a p-value without first finding what was being compared.
  • A result sentence is a summary, not evidence; the design decides what it is worth.

Say it to a friend

“There are two reasons I need stats. One is that I’ll run my own studies and have to work out what the numbers mean. The other is that every paper I read reports its results in a little code — F(2,70) = 8.28, p < .01 — and if I can’t read that code, I just have to believe whatever the author tells me it says.”

Key insight

The symbols in a results sentence are not decoration. Each one answers a specific question, and together they say: here is how big the effect was, here is how much data it came from, and here is how likely a result this extreme would be if nothing were going on.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading “p < .01” as “the effect is big” or “the finding is important”. It says neither. It only says the result would be unusual if there were nothing there — a tiny, useless effect measured on 50 000 people will produce a very small p.

Check yourself

Why does a psychologist who never runs a study still need statistics?
Because the literature reports its findings statistically. Reading a paper critically means being able to tell what the numbers do and do not support — otherwise you are trusting the author’s own summary of their data.
In “F(2,70) = 8.28, p < .01”, which part tells you how much data there was?
The degrees of freedom in brackets, (2, 70). They are built from the number of groups and the number of people, so they carry the size of the study.

🔭 Describing, and going beyond

Descriptive statistics stay in the sample. Inferential statistics leave it.

⬇ builds onstep 1 — why you need any of this⬆ hands upthe reason every later tool exists
  • ▸Descriptive: summarise and organise the results you have.
  • ▸Inferential: draw conclusions about the population from the sample.
  • ▸A descriptive claim makes no leap beyond the cases measured; an inferential one does — which is where it can go wrong even when the arithmetic is flawless.
🪝 memory hookDescribe the sample. Infer the population.

In words

This is the distinction the whole year hangs on. Descriptive statistics summarise and organise what you actually measured: the mean of your 100 students, the histogram of their scores, the percentage who answered yes. Every one of those numbers reports only the cases you measured, so it involves no leap at all. Inferential statistics do something far braver — they use that sample to make a claim about the population it came from, which you did not measure and never will.

  • That is why inference needs probability and descriptives do not.
  • Topics C and D of this page are descriptive. Topics E and F are inferential.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Ask of every number: is this about my sample, or about the world?
  • A sample statistic (x̄, s, p̂) describes. A population parameter (μ, σ, p) is claimed about.
  • You only ever need a test when you are crossing from sample to population.

Say it to a friend

“Saying ‘the 100 students I tested averaged 6.86’ is just a description — I counted, so it’s true. Saying ‘students in general who chew gum score higher’ is a leap, because I never measured students in general. The whole second half of the course is about how big a leap you’re allowed to make.”

Key insight

Every inferential test is a rule for how far you may stretch a sample result before the stretch stops being justified. The arithmetic differs from test to test; the job never does.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Treating a sample mean as if it were the population mean. Your 100 students averaged 6.86; the next 100 will not. The whole machinery of inference exists because that second sample would come out differently.

Check yourself

Is “22% of my sample scored 7” descriptive or inferential?
Descriptive. It is a count of what you observed. It becomes inferential the moment you say “so about 22% of students score 7”.
Why can a descriptive statistic never be “wrong” in the way an inference can?
Because it only claims something about the cases you actually measured. An inference claims something about cases you did not measure, so it can be mistaken even when the arithmetic is flawless.

🏷️ Nominal and ordinal

Qualitative variables: categories, with or without a rank

⬇ builds onstep 3 — the columns of the grid⬆ hands upthe first half of the measurement ladder
  • ▸Nominal: different categories, no “more” or “less”.
  • ▸Ordinal: ranked, but the distances between ranks are not equal.
  • ▸Numbers used as labels (1 = male, 2 = female) are still nominal.
🪝 memory hookNominal names. Ordinal orders.

In words

A qualitative variable records which category someone falls into, not how much of something they have. Nominal means the categories have no order at all — apple, pear, banana; psychologist, doctor, statistician. Ordinal means there is a rank, but the gaps between the ranks are not equal or even known. The lecture’s spider pictures are ordinal: you can line them up from least to most frightening, but the step from the cartoon to the house spider is not the same size as the step from the tarantula to the next one.

  • A mean of a nominal variable is meaningless; a mean of an ordinal one is suspect.
  • The legal rank of a variable is decided by what the numbers mean, not by what SPSS will let you compute.

Rules of the game for this step

  • If swapping two labels changes nothing, the variable is nominal.
  • If the order matters but the gaps do not have to be equal, it is ordinal.
  • Coding categories as numbers never promotes them up the ladder.

Say it to a friend

“Nominal is just names — blue, green, brown; nobody’s eye colour is more than anybody else’s. Ordinal is a queue: first, second, third. You know who’s ahead, but not by how much. The gap between first and second might be a second, and between second and third a minute.”

Key insight

Ordinal data carry order but not arithmetic. That is exactly why they get their own tests — Spearman’s rho, Mann-Whitney — which work on the ranks themselves and never add the values up.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Averaging a Likert item and reporting “mean agreement = 3.4” as if the distance from “disagree” to “neutral” equalled the distance from “neutral” to “agree”. Common in practice, and still technically an ordinal variable being treated as interval.

Check yourself

Is “highest qualification completed: school / bachelor / master / PhD” nominal or ordinal?
Ordinal. There is a clear order, but the amount of extra education between the steps is not equal, so the gaps are not interpretable.
You code nationality as 1 = Dutch, 2 = Belgian, 3 = German. What is the mean of 1.8 telling you?
Nothing. The numbers are labels, so the variable is nominal and the mean is an artefact of the coding — swap the codes and the “mean” changes.

📏 Interval and ratio

Quantitative variables: real numbers, with or without a true zero

⬇ builds onstep 4 — nominal and ordinal⬆ hands uppermission to use means, SDs and z-scores
  • ▸Interval: equal distances, arbitrary zero (IQ, a depression score, °C).
  • ▸Ratio: equal distances and an absolute zero (weight, length, earnings, reaction time).
  • ▸Only ratio scales let you talk about ratios — “twice as”, “half as”.
🪝 memory hookInterval has equal steps. Ratio has a real zero.

In words

A quantitative variable records an amount, and the number means something. Interval scales have equal distances between values — the difference between an IQ of 100 and 110 is the same amount of IQ as between 110 and 120 — but their zero is arbitrary. Ratio scales have everything interval scales have, plus an absolute zero that means “none of it”. That extra zero is what lets you say “twice as much”: 80 kg really is twice 40 kg, but an IQ of 140 is not twice as clever as an IQ of 70.

  • Both are quantitative, so both allow the mean, the SD and the z-score.
  • For almost every test in this course, interval and ratio behave identically.
📌 A note worth having
Temperature is the standard exam example precisely because Celsius is interval and Kelvin is ratio — the same physical quantity, two scales, two sets of permissions.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Ask: does zero on this scale mean “none of it”? Yes → ratio. No → interval.
  • Means and standard deviations need interval level at minimum.
  • Going down the ladder is always allowed; going up never is.

Say it to a friend

“Both are proper numbers. The difference is whether zero means nothing. Zero degrees Celsius isn’t ‘no temperature’, so you can’t say 20° is twice as warm as 10°. Zero kilos is no weight at all, so 20 kg really is twice 10 kg.”

Key insight

The ladder — nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio — is a ladder of permissions. Each rung allows everything the rungs below allow, and one thing more. That is why a ratio variable can always be chopped into categories, and a nominal one can never be rescued into a mean.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Assuming a scale is ratio because its numbers happen to start at zero. A depression questionnaire scored 0–30 has a zero, but that zero means “endorsed no items”, not “no depression at all” — so it is interval, and “twice as depressed” is not a thing you may say.

🧪 Demo 1 · Name the measurement level

Ten variables, four levels. Getting these automatic is worth several marks, because every "which test?" question starts here.

Check yourself

Reaction time in milliseconds — which level, and how do you know?
Ratio. The distances are equal and zero milliseconds means no time elapsed, so 600 ms really is twice 300 ms.
Why can you turn a ratio variable into an ordinal one but not the reverse?
Because going down the ladder throws information away, which is always possible. Going up would require inventing information the data never contained.

📊 How often does each score occur?

The frequency distribution — the table and the picture

⬇ builds onstep 5 — you now have quantitative data⬆ hands upthe shape you will name in the next step
  • ▸Frequency = how many people got each score.
  • ▸Percent = that frequency out of the total; with n = 100 they coincide.
  • ▸Cumulative percent = everyone at this score or below.
🪝 memory hookCount first, average later.

In words

Before any average, before any test, you look at the whole distribution. A frequency distribution is just a count of how often each score occurred, shown either as a table or as a histogram. The lecture uses stress scores for 100 students: 4 people scored 1, two scored 2, and so on up to 10 people scoring 10. The table is exact and lets you read the cumulative percentages straight off. The histogram is approximate and tells you something the table hides — the shape.

  • The table is for exact lookups; the graph is for seeing the shape.
  • In the lecture’s data, 53% of students scored 7 or less — read straight off the cumulative column.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Always plot before you summarise — a mean on an unseen distribution is a guess.
  • Frequencies must add to n, and percentages to 100.

Say it to a friend

“A frequency distribution is just a tally. How many got a 1, how many got a 2, all the way up. Write it as a table and you can look up exact numbers. Draw it as bars and you can see the shape in a second — which is the thing a single average can never show you.”

Key insight

Two datasets can share a mean, a median and a mode and still look nothing alike. The only way to know is to look. That is why every analysis in this course starts with a picture.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Jumping to the mean without plotting. The lecture’s stress data have a long left tail, which pulls the mean (6.86) below the median (7) — a fact the mean alone will never tell you.

The lecture’s own plot

Check yourself

In the lecture’s stress data, what does the cumulative percent of 74 at a score of 8 mean?
74% of the 100 students scored 8 or lower — so 26% scored 9 or 10.
Why does the lecture show the same 100 students as both a table and a graph?
They answer different questions. The table gives exact counts and cumulative percentages; the graph gives shape, which is what you need before choosing how to describe the centre.

📊 Topic C · Describing a pile of numbers · step 2 of 4

🔔 Symmetrical, right-skewed, left-skewed

Naming the shape — and why the name follows the tail

⬇ builds onstep 6 — you have drawn the distribution⬆ hands upwhich measure of centre you should trust
  • ▸Unimodal, bimodal, rectangular — how many peaks?
  • ▸Symmetrical: the two halves mirror each other.
  • ▸Right-skewed: the long tail points right; mean > median > mode.
🪝 memory hookThe tail wags the name.

In words

Once the histogram is in front of you, it gets a name. A distribution can be unimodal (one peak), bimodal (two), or roughly rectangular (no peak at all). If it is unimodal, the next question is whether it is symmetrical or skewed — and the skew is named after the tail, never after the hump. A right-skewed distribution has its long thin tail stretching to the right, even though most of the scores are piled on the left. This trips up almost everyone the first time.

  • Left-skewed: the long tail points left; mean < median < mode.
  • The lecture’s two SPSS plots make the point numerically: in the right-skewed one, mean 6.71 sits above median 6.36; in the left-skewed one, mean 12.29 sits below median 13.64.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Name the skew after the tail, not after where the data pile up.
  • In a skewed distribution the mean is pulled further into the tail than the median — the rule you are expected to use, with rare exceptions.
  • Bimodal usually means two groups mixed together — look for the grouping variable.

Say it to a friend

“The skew points the way the tail points. If most people score low and a few score very high, the tail is on the right — so it’s right-skewed, even though it *looks* like everything’s on the left. And the mean always gets dragged towards the tail, because the mean feels every extreme value.”

Key insight

Shape is not cosmetic. It decides whether the mean is a fair summary, and in Course 2.1 it decides whether you are allowed the parametric test at all — the flowchart’s “distributional assumptions met?” diamond is this question.

The mistake almost everyone makes

🎯 Mode, median, mean

Three answers to “where is the middle?”, and when they disagree

⬇ builds onstep 7 — you know the shape⬆ hands upthe mean, which everything in topic D needs
  • ▸Mode: the most frequent score. Works at every measurement level.
  • ▸Median: the middle score. Needs order, so ordinal and up.
  • ▸Mean: X̄ = ΣX ⁄ N. Needs interval level, and uses every value.
🪝 memory hookMode = most. Median = middle. Mean = shared out.

In words

There are three measures of central tendency and they answer three slightly different questions. The mode is the score that occurs most often — the only one that works for nominal data. The median is the middle score when you line them all up — it counts positions, so extreme values cannot move it. The mean is the sum of all scores divided by how many there are — it uses every value, which is its great strength and its only weakness. In a symmetrical, single-peaked distribution all three coincide; when they disagree, the disagreement itself is information.

  • The mean is the only one an outlier can move a long way.
  • In the lecture’s stress data: mode 7, median 7, mean 6.86 — the small gap is the left tail showing up.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Report the mean for symmetrical data, the median when there is skew or an outlier.
  • The mode is the only legal centre for a nominal variable.
  • If the three disagree, say so — that disagreement is a finding about the shape.

Say it to a friend

“Three ways to say ‘typical’. The mode is the most common one. The median is the one in the middle of the queue. The mean is what everyone would get if you pooled the lot and shared it out equally — which is why one millionaire in the room wrecks the mean and leaves the median alone.”

Key insight

The mean is a balance point: the deviations above it exactly cancel the deviations below it. That property is the reason it is the centre every later formula is built around — the variance, the z-score, the t-test, all of them.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reporting the mean of a badly skewed variable and calling it typical. If the median and the mean are far apart, the mean describes the arithmetic of the data, not the experience of a typical case.

📐 The standard deviation, built from scratch

SS → variance → SD: average spread around the mean

⬇ builds onstep 8 — you have the mean to measure from⬆ hands upthe SD, which the z-score divides by
  • ▸Deviation = X − X̄. These always sum to zero, which is why they get squared.
  • ▸SS = Σ(X − X̄)² — the sum of squares.
  • ▸Variance S² = SS ⁄ (N − 1).
🪝 memory hookDeviate, square, add, divide, root.

In words

The centre is only half the description. Two groups can share a mean of 6 and look completely different — one clustered tightly, one spread wide. The standard deviation measures that spread as a typical distance from the mean — strictly, the square root of the average SQUARED distance — and it is built in three moves. Subtract the mean from each score to get deviations. Square them, because the raw deviations always sum to exactly zero. Add the squares to get the sum of squares, divide by N − 1 to get the variance, and take the square root to get back to the original units.

  • SD = S = √S² — back in the units you started in.
  • The lecture’s six scores 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8: mean 6, SS = 10, S² = 10⁄5 = 2.00, SD = 1.41.
📌 A note worth having
Why N − 1? A sample’s scores sit slightly closer to their own mean than to the population mean, so dividing by N would make the spread look smaller than it really is. Dividing by N − 1 corrects exactly that.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Always compute the mean first — every deviation is measured from it.
  • Check that the deviations sum to zero before squaring; if they do not, the mean is wrong.
  • Divide by N − 1, not N, whenever the data are a sample.

Say it to a friend

“The SD is roughly ‘how far is a typical score from the average?’. You can’t just average the distances, because the ones above and below cancel out to exactly zero. So you square them to kill the minus signs, average those, and then take the square root at the end to undo the squaring.”

Key insight

Everything inferential in this course is a ratio of a difference to a spread. The SD is the first spread you ever compute, and the standard error in topic F is the same idea applied to sample means instead of scores.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reporting the variance when the question asked for the standard deviation. The variance is in squared units — 2.00 “squared stress points” means nothing to a reader. The SD, 1.41, is in the units of the original scale.

Why is squaring the deviations not optional?
Because the deviations from the mean always add to exactly zero, so their average is always zero no matter how spread out the data are. Squaring removes the signs so the distances can accumulate.

🎲 The z-score

How many standard deviations a score sits from the mean

⬇ builds onstep 9 — you have a mean and an SD⬆ hands upa position the normal table can read
  • ▸z = (X − X̄) ⁄ SD.
  • ▸Positive means above the mean, negative below, zero exactly average.
  • ▸The size of z is how many SDs away, so it is directly a measure of unusualness.
🪝 memory hookMinus the mean, divide by the SD.

In words

A raw score on its own is meaningless: 41 is excellent on one test and alarming on another. The z-score fixes that by rewriting every score in the same units. Subtract the mean, so that zero now means average, then divide by the standard deviation, so that one now means “one typical step away”. An IQ of 130 becomes +2; a score one SD below average becomes −1. After that transformation any two scores from any two tests can be laid side by side.

  • Standardising shifts the mean to 0 and the SD to 1; it does not change the shape.
  • IQ 130 with mean 100 and SD 15 gives z = (130 − 100) ⁄ 15 = +2.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Subtract before you divide — the mean goes first, always.
  • Keep the sign: −1.5 and +1.5 are equally unusual but mean opposite things.
  • A z-score is a position, not a probability; turning it into a percentage needs the table.

Say it to a friend

“A z-score is a universal ruler. It says how many standard deviations you are from average. Zero is dead average, +1 is one step above, −2 is two steps below. Once two scores are in z, you can compare them even if one came from an IQ test and the other from a stress questionnaire.”

Key insight

Standardising does not make a distribution normal. It only recentres and rescales it. A skewed distribution turned into z-scores is still exactly as skewed — which is why the normal table in the next step needs the distribution to be normal in the first place.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing by the variance instead of the SD. The SD is in the units of the data, which is what makes z unitless; dividing by the variance leaves a number with no useful meaning.

Check yourself

A depression score of 41 comes from a scale with mean 35 and SD 4. What is its z, and what does the sign tell you?
z = (41 − 35) ⁄ 4 = +1.5. The plus sign says it is above the mean, by one and a half standard deviations.
Two students: one has z = −2.1 on maths, the other z = +0.4 on reading. Who is further from typical?
The first. Distance from typical is the size of z regardless of sign, and 2.1 is larger than 0.4.

🔔 The normal distribution and its table

Turning a position into a percentage of people

⬇ builds onstep 10 — you have a z-score⬆ hands upthe idea of a reference distribution, which inference needs
  • ▸Within ±1 SD lies about 68%; within ±2 SD about 95%.
  • ▸The table converts a z-score into an area, which is a proportion of people.
  • ▸85 and 115 are exactly ∓1 SD from 100, so z = −1 and z = +1.
🪝 memory hook68 inside one SD, 95 inside two.

In words

The normal distribution is a specific bell-shaped curve, and its usefulness is that the proportion of people lying in any stretch of it is known in advance. Within one SD of the mean lies about 68% of everyone; within two, about 95%. The table on the formula sheet makes this exact: feed it a z-score and it gives you the area to the left of that z. The lecture works one question with it — IQ has mean 100 and SD 15, so what percentage of people fall between 85 and 115?

  • The table gives 0.16 in each tail, so 0.32 lies outside and 1 − 0.32 = 0.68 lies between.
  • Answer: 68% — which is why the ±1 SD band is worth memorising.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Draw the curve and shade the region you want before touching the table.
  • The table always gives area to the LEFT of z; everything else is arithmetic on that.
  • Areas are proportions, so they are never negative and never exceed 1.

Say it to a friend

“The bell curve has fixed percentages. About 68% of people are within one SD of the mean, about 95% within two. So for IQ — mean 100, SD 15 — the range 85 to 115 is exactly one SD each way, which means 68% of people are in it. The table is just a finer-grained version of that for z-scores that aren’t whole numbers.”

Key insight

A percentage in the tail is a probability: 2% of people above z = +2 means that if you pick someone at random, there is a 2% chance of getting one. That equivalence — area is probability — is the single idea that makes the whole of topic F possible.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Using the normal table on a distribution that is not normal. The 68% and 95% figures belong to the bell curve alone; on a strongly skewed variable they are simply wrong, however correctly you compute z.

🔄 From an idea to a testable prediction

The empirical cycle, and writing H₀ and Hₐ

⬇ builds onstep 11 — areas under a curve are probabilities⬆ hands upthe two hypotheses every test needs
  • ▸Observation → idea → testable prediction → testing → evaluation → observation.
  • ▸H₀ (null): there is NO difference; the groups are equal.
  • ▸Hₐ (alternative): there IS a difference.
🪝 memory hookH₀ says nothing is happening. Hₐ says something is.

In words

De Groot’s empirical cycle describes how research actually moves: observation produces an idea, the idea is sharpened into a testable prediction, the prediction is tested, the result is evaluated, and the evaluation feeds the next observation. The statistics begin at the third stage, with a translation; stages four and five are where the arithmetic and the decision happen. “Chewing gum improves performance” is an idea, not a hypothesis. The hypothesis pair is H₀, which says there is no difference, and Hₐ, which says there is one — and both are statements about the population, never about your sample.

  • Both are claims about population parameters — μ, not x̄.
  • Two-sided is the default; one-sided only when a result in the other direction would be of no interest at all — and decided before the data.
📌 A note worth having
One-sided testing is flagged in the lecture with “caution!!” for a reason: it halves the p-value, so it doubles the ease of a significant result, and it is only legitimate when a result in the other direction would be meaningless rather than merely unwelcome.

Rules of the game for this step

  • H₀ always carries the equals sign. It is the claim you test, not the one you hope for.
  • Write the hypotheses before you look at the data, not after.
  • Choose one- or two-sided before collecting data too — switching afterwards is cheating.

Say it to a friend

“Before testing anything I have to write down two sentences. The null says ‘nothing’s going on, the groups are the same’. The alternative says ‘something is’. Then the whole test is about whether my data are too weird to live in the null’s world. And both sentences are about everyone, not about the hundred people I actually measured.”

Key insight

H₀ is testable precisely because it is specific: “μ = 6.9” tells you exactly what results to expect. “μ ≠ 6.9” tells you almost nothing, which is why the null is the one you can build a distribution around and the alternative is only ever accepted by elimination.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Writing the hypotheses about the sample — H₀: x̄ = 6.9. The sample mean is a number you measured; there is nothing to hypothesise about it. Hypotheses are always about the unmeasured population.

H₀: μ = 6.2. Hₐ: μ ≠ 6.2. Two-sided, because the method could plausibly make things worse as well as better.
Why is the null hypothesis the one that gets tested rather than the one you believe?
Because it is specific enough to predict from. “No difference” pins the population value down exactly, so you can work out how surprising your data would be if it were true.

⚗️ Two equal groups, and a distribution of differences

Randomisation, the IV and the DV, and why any difference might be noise

⬇ builds onstep 12 — you have H₀ and Hₐ⬆ hands upthe reason a test statistic is needed at all
  • ▸Random assignment makes the two groups equivalent on average on everything unmeasured — so any leftover difference is chance, not bias.
  • ▸Independent variable: what you manipulate — gum or no gum.
  • ▸Dependent variable: what you measure — performance.
🪝 memory hookChance alone makes differences. The test asks: this big?

In words

To test the gum hypothesis you take a sample and split it into two groups using random assignment — the technical name for the lecture’s “two equal groups”. Random assignment is what makes the groups equivalent on average on everything you did not measure, which is what licenses a causal claim later. One group chews gum (that is the independent variable, the thing you manipulate), and both groups are measured on performance (the dependent variable, the thing you observe). Then comes the uncomfortable part: even if gum does nothing at all, the two group means will differ, because people differ.

  • Even under H₀ the difference between the groups is almost never exactly zero.
  • So the real question is not “is there a difference?” but “is this difference bigger than chance alone would produce?”

Rules of the game for this step

  • Random assignment is what buys causality; random sampling is what buys generalisability. They are different things.
  • The IV is the cause being tested, the DV is the effect being measured — never swap them.
  • A difference of zero is not expected even when H₀ is true.

Say it to a friend

“I split people randomly into two groups so the groups start off the same. One gets gum, one doesn’t, and I measure how they do. But here’s the thing — even if gum does nothing, the two averages won’t come out identical, because people aren’t identical. So the question is never ‘is there a difference’ but ‘is this difference too big to be luck’.”

Key insight

The distribution of difference scores on slide 38 is the whole of inference in one picture: it shows what differences chance alone produces, with the extreme tails shaded. Your observed difference is a single arrow landing somewhere on that curve, and the shaded region is the answer.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Confusing random sampling with random assignment. Sampling at random from the population lets you generalise; assigning at random to conditions lets you say the gum caused it. A study can have one without the other, and most have neither in full.

Check yourself

In the gum study, what are the independent and dependent variables?
The independent variable is whether a person chews gum — the manipulated condition. The dependent variable is their performance score, which is what you measure.
If gum truly had no effect, what would you expect the difference between the two group means to be?
Close to zero, but essentially never exactly zero. Sampling variation alone produces differences, and their typical size is what the test compares yours against.

⚖️ The courtroom, and the two errors

Type I, Type II, α, β and power

⬇ builds onstep 13 — differences arise by chance⬆ hands upthe significance level every test needs
  • ▸Type I error: rejecting H₀ when it is true — a false alarm, probability α.
  • ▸Type II error: failing to reject H₀ when it is false — a miss, probability β.
  • ▸α is chosen by you before the study; .05 is the convention.
🪝 memory hookα convicts the innocent. β frees the guilty.

In words

The legal analogy on slide 39 is the cleanest way into this. A court starts from the presumption of innocence — that is H₀ — and the evidence either convicts or fails to. Two mistakes are possible and they are not symmetrical. Convicting an innocent person is a Type I error, and the probability you are willing to accept for it is α, which you choose, usually .05. Letting a guilty person go is a Type II error, with probability β. Power is 1 − β: the chance of correctly convicting someone who is guilty.

  • Power = 1 − β, the chance of detecting an effect that is really there.
  • Lowering α makes false alarms rarer and misses commoner; a bigger sample raises power without touching α.

Rules of the game for this step

  • Set α before you see the data. Choosing it afterwards is not a test.
  • “Not significant” means not enough evidence, never “H₀ is true”.
  • The two error rates trade off against each other at fixed sample size.

Say it to a friend

“It’s a trial. H₀ is ‘innocent’. I can get it wrong two ways: convict someone innocent, or let someone guilty walk. Alpha is how often I’m willing to convict the innocent — usually 5%. Power is how often I catch the guilty. And a verdict of ‘not guilty’ never means ‘proven innocent’; it just means the evidence wasn’t enough.”

Key insight

You can always drive one error rate to zero — convict nobody, or convict everybody. The art is the trade-off, and the only way to improve both at once is to collect more evidence. That is why sample size is a design decision, not an afterthought.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading p > .05 as evidence that H₀ is true. A failure to reject is a failure to convict: it is entirely compatible with a real effect that the study was simply too small to detect.

🧪 The six steps, run once in full

The chewing-gum z-test from hypotheses to conclusion

⬇ builds onstep 14 — you have α and the two errors⬆ hands upa procedure identical for every test in the course
  • ▸H₀: μ(gum) = 6.9, Hₐ: μ(gum) ≠ 6.9, two-sided, α = .05.
  • ▸N = 100 gum-chewers, x̄ = 7.5; the population σ is known to be 2.3.
  • ▸The standard deviation of the sampling distribution is σ ⁄ √N = 2.3 ⁄ 10 = 0.23.
🪝 memory hookSix steps. Only the middle three change.

In words

Every statistical test in this course follows the same six steps, and the lecture walks the z-test through all of them. State H₀ and Hₐ. Choose the procedure and the decision rule. Draw the sample. Find the reference distribution — the distribution of sample means if H₀ were true — and compute the test statistic. Find the p-value, which is the probability of a result this extreme or more, assuming H₀ is true. Then decide. Steps 2, 4 and 5 are the ones that change from test to test — which procedure, which reference distribution, which table. Steps 1, 3 and 6 are identical every time.

  • z = (7.5 − 6.9) ⁄ 0.23 = 2.61.
  • The table gives P(Z < 2.61) = .9955, so one tail is .0045 and two-sided p ≈ .009 — well under .05, so H₀ is rejected.
📌 A note worth having
The critical-value route gives the same decision with less arithmetic: for a two-sided test at α = .05 the critical region starts at ±1.96, and the observed z of 2.61 is past it.

Rules of the game for this step

  • The p-value is computed ASSUMING H₀ is true. It is never the probability that H₀ is true.
  • Reject when p < α, otherwise do not reject — and say “do not reject”, not “accept”.
  • The reference distribution is a distribution of sample means, not of individual scores.

Say it to a friend

“Every test is the same six moves. Write the two hypotheses, pick your test and your 5% rule, get your data, work out how far your result sits from what the null predicts — in standard-error units — then look up how rare that is, and decide. The gum study came out 2.61 standard errors above the null, and that happens less than 1% of the time by chance, so the null goes.”

Key insight

Sample means vary far less than individual scores — the spread shrinks by a factor of √N. That is the whole reason a sample mean of 7.5 can be strong evidence against a population mean of 6.9, even though a single person scoring 7.5 would be entirely unremarkable.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing by the sample SD of 2.1 rather than the known population σ of 2.3. The z-test is defined by σ being known; the moment you have to estimate it from the sample, the test becomes a t-test.

🧭 Which test does this need?

Last year’s four tests, told apart by two questions

⬇ builds onstep 15 — you can run the procedure⬆ hands upthe rest of Course 2.1, which adds more cells to the same grid
  • ▸H₀: μ = C with σ known → z-test, z = (x̄ − C) ⁄ (σ⁄√N).
  • ▸H₀: μ = C with σ unknown → one-sample t-test, t = (x̄ − C) ⁄ (s⁄√N).
  • ▸H₀: μ₁ = μ₂, two separate groups → independent-samples t-test.
🪝 memory hookHow many groups, and do I know σ?

In words

Slide 52 collects everything from last year into one table, and it is the slide worth photographing. Four tests, and two questions separate them. First: how many groups — one mean compared against a fixed number, or two means compared against each other? Second: do you know the population standard deviation? If you do, it is a z-test; if you have to estimate it from your sample, it is a t. And if your two measurements come from the same people, it is a paired test, always.

  • H₀: δ = 0, the same people measured twice → paired-samples t-test on the difference scores.
  • C is whatever number the claim names — zero, an IQ of 100, last year’s mean of 6.9.

Rules of the game for this step

  • One group or two? Then: is σ known? Those two answers pick the test.
  • Same people twice is always paired — never run an independent-samples test on repeated measures.
  • In practice σ is almost never known, which is why t-tests are far more common than z-tests.

Say it to a friend

“Four tests, two questions. Am I comparing one average to a fixed number, or two averages to each other? And do I actually know the population’s spread, or am I guessing it from my data? Known spread means z, guessed means t. And if it’s the same people measured twice, it’s always the paired one.”

Key insight

The paired t-test is not a separate idea. You subtract each person’s two scores to get one difference per person, and then run a one-sample test on those differences against zero. Two columns become one, and the problem shrinks to a test you already know.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Running an independent-samples t-test on before-and-after data. Pairing removes the differences between people, which is usually most of the noise — ignoring it throws away the study’s power for nothing.

Check yourself

You measure 30 people’s anxiety before and after therapy. Which test, and what is H₀?
A paired-samples t-test, because it is the same people twice. H₀: δ = 0 — the mean difference score in the population is zero.
Why are z-tests rare in practice despite being the ones textbooks teach first?
Because they require the population standard deviation to be known in advance, which almost never happens. As soon as you estimate σ from your own sample, the correct test is a t-test.

Question 13 · topic C · step 9 · the SD

  • d.s² = 1.41 and s = 2.00
